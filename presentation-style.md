# Presentation style

How to build a deck for Zachary Hansen. Everything below describes what goes on
screen. The deck is a backdrop for a person talking, so it is never a handout and
never explains itself.

## The rule everything else follows

One idea per slide, and the slide carries that idea and nothing more. If the
slide contains the full argument, the presenter has no job and the room reads
ahead instead of listening.

## Text

- No bullet lists. Ever.
- Most slides have no heading at all. Add one only when the slide would be
  ambiguous without it.
- On-screen text is a handful of words, usually under ten. A short line, not a
  sentence that hedges.
- Never a slide of prose. If something needs a paragraph, it is spoken.
- A slide may run past 50 words when it has to, but then something in it must
  stand out: a bold or accent-coloured phrase, less than half the text, that
  the eye lands on first.
- Quotations are the exception and get a slide to themselves, unattributed on
  screen.

## Single-element slides

Whole slides that are one thing, with no caption, no title, no frame:

- An image alone, full width.
- A video alone, full bleed.
- A link alone, centred, clickable.
- A photograph as the entire slide, with text sitting in whatever part of the
  frame is empty. If the image already has a title burned into it, the slide gets
  no heading.

## Charts and figures

Three parts, top to bottom:

1. A neutral grey title naming only what is plotted, e.g. "Camera and body-worn
   sensor agreement, by motion excursion". It states the axes and makes no claim.
2. The chart.
3. A takeaway line in bold, which is the claim, with the load-bearing phrase
   emphasized in the accent colour. "Cameras match body-worn sensors, but only
   for *large motions*." "A *150-fold gap* between the best and the worst model."

The title and the takeaway never say the same thing. Where the chart deserves a
few silent seconds, hold the takeaway back on a click.

## Order

Show the thing before explaining it. The raw artifact comes first and the
aggregate comes second: footage of the system running before any chart about the
system, a real logged exchange before a bar chart of thousands of them, three
worked examples before the slide that counts them.

## Build-ups

Build by repeating a near-identical slide rather than by animating one. Same
layout, one more column filled in each time, everything else holding still so the
room sees exactly what changed. Empty slots keep their boxes so nothing shifts.

## Motion

Rare and reserved. Pick the two or three moments in the deck that earn an
animation and let them be the only ones. Everything else is static. Any new
animation dilutes the ones already there, so it has to displace one.

## Numbers

A number that matters gets the slide. Large numeral, a line of caption under it,
nothing else. Two contrasting numbers side by side is the strongest version of
this.

## Chrome

None. No slide numbers, no section labels in the corner, no footers, no captions
under figures, no navigation buttons, no agenda or outline slide.

Structure does the orienting instead:

- Numbered project dividers, an eyebrow like "Project 2" over a bold title, used
  as breath marks between bodies of work.
- Chapter openers, usually a photograph, whose one line of text names what is
  coming next.

## Rhythm

A section runs setup, then evidence, then one pointed line. Pacing is deliberately
uneven: a two-word slide that lands in a few seconds sits next to one the room
looks at for a minute and a half.

Somewhere in the deck, put a question directly to the room and leave the screen
holding still while they answer.

Close by calling back to the line the deck opened with.
